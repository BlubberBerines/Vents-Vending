# PART 1 — GAME VISION & IDENTITY

## 1.1 High Concept

Venting & Vending is a first-person sci-fi black-market business simulator set aboard a gigantic, overcrowded intergalactic space station.

The player begins as an insignificant nobody living inside the maintenance ventilation system of a massive alien metropolis.

With practically no money, no connections, no legal identity worth mentioning and a questionable collection of equipment, the player starts a small underground operation manufacturing and selling strange, fictional alien substances.

Through crafting, trading, exploration, stealth, negotiation and increasingly ambitious business decisions, the player gradually builds a criminal enterprise.

What begins as a makeshift laboratory hidden behind a rusty ventilation grate eventually becomes a sophisticated interstellar operation involving automated manufacturing, hired alien workers, private spaceships, multiple distribution locations and relationships with corrupt galactic officials.

The ultimate objective is to become one of the most influential black-market operators in the known galaxy.

The player should feel like an ordinary person who has accidentally stumbled into an enormous and ridiculous criminal opportunity.

## 1.2 The Core Fantasy

The player fantasy is:

"I started with nothing, lived in a ventilation shaft, sold weird alien products to complete strangers, and somehow ended up running a massive intergalactic empire."

The progression from absolute poverty to excessive wealth is central to the game's appeal.

Players should experience:

- The satisfaction of making their first successful sale.
- The excitement of discovering a profitable new product.
- The anxiety of conducting an illegal transaction near security.
- The pleasure of upgrading their equipment.
- The pride of transforming an embarrassing hideout into a proper operation.
- The freedom of eventually owning and customising a spaceship.
- The absurdity of managing employees, customers and galactic politics.

## 1.3 Genre and Gameplay Positioning

The game combines several genres:

| Genre | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Business simulator | Production, pricing, sales and profit |
| First-person simulator | Direct physical interaction |
| Stealth | Avoiding security and managing risk |
| Exploration | Discovering districts, customers and resources |
| Management | Staff, automation and logistics |
| Progression RPG | Unlocks, reputation and equipment |

The game must not become a traditional combat-focused action game.

The player is a business operator, not a soldier.

Danger comes primarily from operating illegally in an environment controlled by authorities, rival operators and unpredictable aliens.

## 1.4 Design Pillars

All gameplay and production decisions should support five pillars.

### Pillar 1: Satisfying Physical Interactions

Players should physically interact with the majority of important gameplay objects.

Examples:

- Picking up containers.
- Opening storage units.
- Pouring liquids.
- Operating machinery.
- Mixing fictional substances.
- Packaging finished products.
- Inserting ingredients into equipment.
- Loading products into storage.
- Opening and closing ventilation hatches.

Menus should support gameplay rather than replace it.

### Pillar 2: Risk Versus Reward

The most profitable opportunities should involve increased risk.

Players must decide whether to:

- Sell somewhere safe for less money.
- Take a higher-value order in a dangerous district.
- Carry more inventory and risk losing it.
- Bribe an official.
- Pay for better security.
- Operate during high-security periods.

### Pillar 3: Humorous Science Fiction

The world must constantly communicate that this is a ridiculous universe.

Aliens should have strange personalities, unusual motivations and absurd dialogue.

Humour should emerge naturally from gameplay, environmental storytelling and character behaviour.

### Pillar 4: Meaningful Progression

Every successful business activity should contribute towards a visible objective.

The player should always have something interesting to work towards.

### Pillar 5: A Living World

Customers, workers, security drones and background aliens should make the station feel populated.

Even when the player is not interacting with them, they should behave as if they have their own lives.

# PART 2 — WORLD BUILDING & STORY

## 2.1 The Galactic Setting

The game takes place within a technologically advanced but socially dysfunctional galactic civilisation.

Numerous alien species share enormous space stations, commercial hubs and interconnected trade networks.

The Galactic Federation officially maintains order, regulates trade and prevents dangerous technologies from circulating.

In reality, the system is bureaucratic, corrupt, inefficient and full of opportunities for exploitation.

Illegal markets operate in abandoned industrial sectors, maintenance corridors, forgotten docking bays and hidden commercial districts.

The player begins aboard one of the largest stations in the system.

## 2.2 The Main Location: The Citadel

**Working name:** The Grand Exchange / Citadel-9.

The station should feel enormous.

It contains:

- Luxury residential districts.
- Commercial plazas.
- Industrial manufacturing sectors.
- Public teleportation gates.
- Alien entertainment venues.
- Docking facilities.
- Maintenance infrastructure.
- Government offices.
- Security checkpoints.
- Abandoned and restricted sectors.

The player initially has access to only a tiny portion of this environment.

The rest of the station should be visible through windows, distant architecture, signage, background traffic and inaccessible doors.

This creates the impression of a much larger world without requiring every area to be playable.

## 2.3 The Galactic Federation

The Federation is the primary authority.

Its responsibilities include:

- Maintaining station security.
- Regulating trade.
- Controlling restricted substances.
- Monitoring teleportation networks.
- Collecting taxes and commercial fees.
- Managing immigration and identity registration.

Its employees range from genuinely competent officers to incompetent bureaucrats.

The Federation should be threatening but frequently ridiculous.

Example dialogue:

"Citizen, please remain stationary while I determine whether your existence violates subsection 84-B."

"Your ventilation access permit expired 312 rotations ago."

"Unlicensed molecular condensation detected. This is a serious administrative inconvenience."

## 2.4 Story Structure

The game should have a light overarching narrative rather than a heavily scripted campaign.

### Chapter 1: Nobody

The player arrives at the station with almost nothing.

A contact introduces them to the maintenance network and explains how to access a forgotten ventilation chamber.

The player establishes their first illegal operation.

### Chapter 2: Small-Time Operator

The player builds a customer network, discovers profitable recipes and upgrades their equipment.

They begin attracting the attention of station security and other operators.

### Chapter 3: Station Reputation

The player unlocks new districts and develops relationships with alien groups.

They gain access to better equipment and larger orders.

### Chapter 4: The Junker

The player acquires an old, partially broken spaceship.

This becomes a mobile headquarters and production facility.

### Chapter 5: Independent Operation

The player establishes automated production, hires workers and begins supplying customers across multiple locations.

### Chapter 6: The Galactic Hustle

The player becomes a major black-market figure.

They can influence officials, exploit political relationships and establish a network of businesses.

The final chapter should provide an endgame sandbox rather than forcing a single ending.

# PART 3 — PLAYER CHARACTER & MOVEMENT

## 3.1 Player Perspective

The entire game uses a first-person perspective.

The camera should feel grounded, responsive and immersive.

The player should see:

- Their hands during interactions.
- Objects being picked up and manipulated.
- Equipment animations.
- Their body when looking down, if feasible.
- Appropriate camera transitions when crawling.
- Environmental movement and effects.

Avoid excessive camera movement, artificial head bob or unnecessary screen shake.

## 3.2 Movement Modes

The player has five main movement states.

| State | Description |
|---|---|
| Normal | Standard first-person walking |
| Sprinting | Faster movement |
| Crouching | Reduced height and speed |
| Crawling | Vent-specific movement |
| Interacting | Special movement restrictions |

### Initial Movement Tuning

These are starting values to be tested, not final balancing requirements.

| Property | Starting value |
|---|---:|
| Walking speed | 4.2 m/s |
| Sprint speed | 6.2 m/s |
| Crouch speed | 2.2 m/s |
| Crawl speed | 1.6 m/s |
| Jump height | 1.0 m |
| Gravity | -20 m/s² |
| Interaction distance | 2.5 m |
| Normal camera height | 1.65 m |
| Crouch camera height | 1.05 m |
| Crawl camera height | 0.55 m |

### Implementation

Use Unity's CharacterController for initial movement.

Create separate scripts:

- PlayerInputHandler.
- PlayerMovement.
- PlayerCamera.
- PlayerStateController.
- PlayerCrouch.
- VentCrawler.

Movement states should be managed centrally through an enum or state machine.

Do not allow sprinting or jumping inside restricted vent sections unless a future level mechanic explicitly requires it.

## 3.3 Interaction System

The interaction system is one of the most important technical foundations.

Every interactable object should implement a shared interface.

Objects include:

- Machines.
- Storage containers.
- Doors.
- Hatches.
- Customers.
- Shop terminals.
- Teleporters.
- Upgrade stations.
- Beds.
- Vehicles.

The player aims at an object.

A raycast identifies the target.

The UI displays the relevant interaction prompt.

Example:

[ E ] Open storage

[ E ] Operate mixer

[ E ] Speak to customer

[ E ] Enter ventilation shaft

Interactions should be consistent throughout the game.

# PART 4 — THE VENT NETWORK

## 4.1 Core Concept

The ventilation system is the player's first home, transportation network and primary stealth mechanic.

It runs behind walls, beneath floors and above the ceilings of the commercial districts.

It should feel like a hidden world existing directly behind the public environment.

## 4.2 Vent Environment Design

Ventilation spaces should contain:

- Rusty metal walls.
- Pipes.
- Cable bundles.
- Fans.
- Steam outlets.
- Electrical junctions.
- Maintenance lights.
- Access ladders.
- Service platforms.
- Hidden storage locations.

The vents should be claustrophobic but navigable.

The player should occasionally hear aliens talking, music playing or machines operating on the other side of the walls.

## 4.3 Vent Gameplay

The player can:

1. Approach an accessible hatch.
2. Open the hatch.
3. Enter the ventilation network.
4. Crawl through connected sections.
5. Observe public areas through grates.
6. Reach different exit points.
7. Open a grate.
8. Drop into the environment.

Some vents should only be accessible from one direction.

Later upgrades can unlock additional routes.

## 4.4 Vent Hazards

Potential hazards include:

- Rotating fans.
- Electrical faults.
- Hot steam.
- Toxic alien residue.
- Collapsing sections.
- Security scanning devices.

For the initial release, hazards should be limited to two or three well-designed examples.

## 4.5 Vent Navigation

Create the ventilation network using modular pieces.

Each section should have standard connection points.

Suggested prefab types:

- Straight corridor.
- Left turn.
- Right turn.
- Vertical shaft.
- T-junction.
- Four-way junction.
- Fan corridor.
- Maintenance room.
- Exit grate.
- Access hatch.

Use manually constructed level layouts rather than procedural generation.

The vent network should be authored as a connected level graph.

# PART 5 — THE HIDEOUT & PROPERTY PROGRESSION

## 5.1 Starting Hideout

The player begins in a forgotten maintenance chamber.

It is dirty, poorly lit and barely functional.

Initial equipment:

- One sleeping area.
- One storage container.
- One crude mixing station.
- One manual packaging surface.
- One basic ventilation exit.

The hideout should feel personal and gradually become more impressive.

## 5.2 Hideout Upgrades

Upgrades should be purchased using cosmic credits.

### Tier 1: Improvised

- Cardboard storage.
- Homemade mixer.
- Basic lighting.
- Manual packaging.

### Tier 2: Functional

- Proper ingredient storage.
- Improved mixing equipment.
- Additional workbench.
- Better lighting.
- Basic security camera.

### Tier 3: Professional

- Automated packaging.
- Advanced refinery.
- Larger storage.
- Additional production equipment.
- Improved ventilation access.

### Tier 4: Industrial

- Automated matter collectors.
- Multiple production stations.
- Advanced research equipment.
- Worker access.
- Security monitoring.

### Tier 5: Executive

This tier should eventually transition the player towards spaceship ownership.

## 5.3 Property Management

Later in the game, players should be able to acquire:

- Additional maintenance rooms.
- Hidden commercial units.
- Abandoned industrial facilities.
- Private docking spaces.
- Warehouse facilities.

Each property should have a purpose.

Avoid adding properties that simply duplicate the same functionality.

# PART 6 — THE PRODUCTION & CRAFTING SYSTEM

## 6.1 Design Philosophy

Crafting is the heart of the gameplay loop.

It should feel physical, deliberate and rewarding.

The player should not simply click a recipe and wait for a timer.

Instead, machines should have visible stages of operation.

The production system should support:

- Ingredient collection.
- Storage.
- Ingredient measurement.
- Liquid transfer.
- Mixing.
- Refining.
- Stabilisation.
- Quality inspection.
- Packaging.
- Product storage.

All substances in the game are fictional alien materials with invented properties.

## 6.2 Production Equipment

### Equipment 1: Basic Mixing Station

The first production machine.

Visual features:

- Metal workbench.
- Glass mixing vessel.
- Manual stirring mechanism.
- Ingredient slots.
- Temperature display.
- Simple pressure gauge.

Player interactions:

- Place ingredients.
- Pour liquids.
- Activate mixing.
- Monitor progress.
- Collect the finished material.

### Equipment 2: Molecular Refinery

Unlocks advanced recipes.

Features:

- Glowing containment chamber.
- Rotating molecular components.
- Cooling system.
- Digital control panel.
- Stability monitoring.

### Equipment 3: Stabilisation Chamber

Improves product quality.

Features:

- Containment glass.
- Temperature regulation.
- Energy controls.
- Pressure indicators.

### Equipment 4: Packaging Machine

Turns finished material into sellable units.

Features:

- Empty container loading.
- Product filling.
- Sealing.
- Labelling.
- Finished product collection.

### Equipment 5: Automated Production Unit

Late-game equipment.

Allows the player to configure recipes and automate manufacturing.

Products should have:

- Unique appearances.
- Distinct production requirements.
- Different customer preferences.
- Different values.
- Different stability characteristics.
- Different levels of risk.
- Unique packaging.

The catalogue should grow with the player's progression.

The initial commercial release should aim for approximately 10–12 products, with three available during the opening gameplay section.

## 7.4 Recipe Data Architecture

Recipes should be data-driven.

Each recipe should contain:

- Recipe ID.
- Product output.
- Required ingredients.
- Ingredient quantities.
- Production duration.
- Equipment requirement.
- Minimum skill or unlock requirement.
- Base quality.
- Possible quality modifiers.
- Failure conditions.

The game should support recipes being added through ScriptableObjects.

## 7.5 Product Quality

Products should have four quality levels:

| Quality | Description |
|---|---|
| Poor | Unstable, visibly flawed |
| Standard | Normal production quality |
| Premium | Improved quality and value |
| Exceptional | Rare high-quality output |

Quality should be affected by:

- Ingredient quality.
- Correct production sequence.
- Machine condition.
- Production equipment.
- Stabilisation performance.
- Optional player skill bonuses.

Poor-quality products should not always be useless. Certain alien customers may specifically prefer unstable or unusual products.

## 7.6 Production Failure

Manufacturing should occasionally create problems.

Potential failures:

- Overheating.
- Pressure imbalance.
- Incorrect ingredient quantity.
- Unstable reaction.
- Containment leakage.
- Machine breakdown.

Failures should create entertaining situations rather than constantly punishing the player.

Examples:

- A mixing station starts shaking.
- A container floats away.
- The machine releases colourful smoke.
- The product becomes an unexpected variation.
- A small explosion covers the room in glowing slime.

Failures should be recoverable unless the player deliberately ignores serious warnings.

The alien population is one of the game's biggest opportunities for personality and humour.

Every major species should have distinct anatomy, movement, dialogue, economic preferences and social behaviour.

# PART 9 — CUSTOMER AI & SOCIAL SYSTEMS

## 9.1 Customer Behaviour

Customers should have daily routines.

A basic customer lifecycle:

1. Spawn in the district.
2. Travel to a destination.
3. Browse or wait.
4. Approach a seller.
5. Initiate a conversation.
6. Request a product.
7. Complete a transaction.
8. React to the result.
9. Leave the area.

Some customers should occasionally:

- Cancel an order.
- Change their minds.
- Ask for a discount.
- Request a specific quality.
- Complain about a product.
- Refer another customer.
- Become suspicious.

## 9.2 Customer Preferences

Each customer should have:

- Preferred product categories.
- Maximum spending amount.
- Quality expectations.
- Patience.
- Suspicion.
- Relationship value.

The customer system should support personalised buying behaviour.

## 9.3 Reputation

The player should have two reputation layers.

**General reputation**

Represents the player's overall standing in the black market.

**Species reputation**

Represents how individual alien groups perceive the player.

Positive reputation can unlock:

- Better orders.
- Exclusive customers.
- Rare ingredients.
- Discounts.
- New areas.
- Special dialogue.

Negative reputation can create:

- Higher prices.
- Reduced demand.
- Suspicious customers.
- Rival interference.

# PART 10 — DISTRIBUTION & SALES

## 10.1 Early-Game Sales

Initially, the player must physically visit customers.

They leave the hideout through a vent and enter the plaza.

Customers can be approached directly.

The player can offer available products and negotiate simple prices.

## 10.2 Sales Interaction

A typical sale:

1. Approach the customer.
2. Start interaction.
3. View the requested product.
4. Offer a quantity.
5. Confirm price.
6. Complete the exchange.
7. Receive credits.
8. Update reputation.

Sales should be fast and satisfying.

Avoid lengthy dialogue for routine transactions.

## 10.3 Order System

Later customers can place orders.

Orders should contain:

- Customer.
- Product.
- Quantity.
- Required quality.
- Deadline.
- Payment.
- Reputation reward.
- Failure penalty.

## 10.4 Pricing

The player should have control over product pricing.

Prices should be influenced by:

- Base product value.
- Quality.
- Customer preference.
- District demand.
- Reputation.
- Scarcity.

The economy should be understandable.

Avoid creating a complicated simulation that is difficult to balance.

# PART 11 — GALACTIC SECURITY & STEALTH

## 11.1 Security Types

The initial game should include two security types.

### Patrol Drone

- Follows predefined patrol routes.
- Scans nearby areas.
- Investigates suspicious activity.
- Pursues identified players.

### Federation Inspector

- Walks around commercial districts.
- Interacts with citizens.
- Conducts occasional inspections.
- Responds to illegal activity.

## 11.2 Detection System

Security should detect players based on:

- Distance.
- Line of sight.
- Suspicious behaviour.
- Restricted inventory.
- Security level.
- Previous incidents.

Use Unity physics raycasts for line-of-sight checks.

## 11.3 Alert States

Security has four states:

**Unaware**

Normal patrol.

**Suspicious**

Investigating something unusual.

**Searching**

Looking for the player's last known position.

**Pursuing**

Actively chasing the player.

## 11.4 Escape Mechanics

Players can:

- Run.
- Break line of sight.
- Enter vents.
- Hide in maintenance spaces.

# PART 15 — ART DIRECTION & VISUAL BIBLE

## 15.1 Overall Style

The art direction is:

**Low-Poly Grungy / Stylized 3D.**

The game should use relatively simple geometry with detailed material work, strong lighting and carefully controlled atmosphere.

It must not look unfinished or cheaply low-poly.

The goal is intentional stylisation.

## 15.2 Geometry

Characteristics:

- Angular silhouettes.
- Simplified character anatomy.
- Exaggerated proportions.
- Modular architecture.
- Limited unnecessary geometry.
- Readable silhouettes.
- Chunky environmental props.

Use polygon detail where it matters visually.

Don't waste time modelling tiny objects players will rarely see.

## 15.3 Textures

Textures should communicate wear and use.

Environmental materials include:

- Scratched metal.
- Dirty concrete.
- Rust.
- Grease.
- Oil.
- Faded paint.
- Worn plastic.
- Industrial grime.

The public plaza should contrast with the maintenance network.

It can use cleaner materials, brighter colours and more polished architecture.

## 15.4 Colour Palette

### Maintenance areas

- Dark grey.
- Rust brown.
- Dirty yellow.
- Desaturated green.
- Muted orange.
- Industrial blue.

### Public plaza

- Neon cyan.
- Purple.
- Magenta.
- Warm orange.
- Deep blue.
- Metallic silver.

### Alien products

Each product should have a distinctive colour identity.

For example:

- Glarb-Juice: neon green.
- Chronos-Dust: purple.
- Void-Paste: black and violet.
- Nebula Nectar: pink.
- Quasar Fizz: orange and blue.
- Quantum Jelly: translucent cyan.

## 15.5 Lighting

Use URP lighting features carefully.

Important elements:

- Baked lighting where practical.
- Selective realtime lights.
- Emissive materials.
- Bloom.
- Fog.
- Localised coloured lighting.
- Flickering light sources.
- Strong directional contrast.

Avoid filling every environment with bright lights.

Darkness should help create atmosphere.

## 15.6 Character Art

Alien characters should be immediately recognisable.

Prioritise:

- Distinctive silhouettes.
- Exaggerated heads.
- Unusual anatomy.
- Memorable colour palettes.
- Readable facial expressions.
- Strong idle animations.

Use shared rigs where practical.

Not every alien needs an entirely bespoke animation system.

## 15.7 Animation

Important animations:

- Walking.
- Running.
- Idle.
- Talking.
- Buying.
- Handing over products.
- Receiving products.
- Inspecting objects.
- Reacting to surprise.
- Suspicious behaviour.
- Security scanning.

Environmental animation:

- Vent fans.
- Machinery.
- Pipes.
- Moving signs.
- Alien advertisements.
- Holographic displays.

## 15.8 UI Style

The interface should resemble a mixture of industrial equipment and futuristic commercial software.

Characteristics:

- Dark translucent panels.
- Angular containers.
- Bright accent colours.
- Simple icons.
- Compact typography.
- Clear money indicators.
- Minimal clutter.

The UI should feel like it belongs in the world.

## 18.4 Data-Driven Design

Use ScriptableObjects for static definitions.

Examples:

- ItemDefinition.
- RecipeDefinition.
- SpeciesDefinition.
- CustomerDefinition.
- ProductDefinition.
- UpgradeDefinition.
- DistrictDefinition.

Runtime state should be stored separately.

For example, an ItemDefinition defines what a product is, while the player's inventory save data stores how many units they currently own.

## 18.5 Saving Architecture

The save system should preserve:

- Player position.
- Currency.
- Inventory.
- Equipment.
- Recipes.
- Upgrades.
- Reputation.
- Customer relationships.
- Property ownership.
- Production states.
- Business progression.

Use JSON serialisation initially.

Use stable IDs rather than relying on Unity instance references.

Save files should include a version number to support future updates.

Implement:

- Manual saving.
- Automatic saving.
- Save-on-exit where safe.
- Multiple save slots.
- Backup saves.
- Basic corruption handling.

## 18.6 Performance

Target:

- 60 FPS at 1080p on the minimum supported hardware.
- Stable frame pacing.
- Reasonable memory usage.
- Fast loading.
- Efficient AI updates.

Use:

- Occlusion culling.
- Baked lighting.
- Object pooling.
- LODs where needed.
- Efficient materials.
- Limited realtime shadows.
- AI update throttling.
- Scene-based loading.

Do not optimise blindly. Profile actual bottlenecks.

## 18.7 Build Configuration

The initial release should support:

- Windows 10/11 64-bit.
- Keyboard and mouse.
- Common controller layouts where feasible.
- 16:9 and ultrawide display support.
- Configurable graphics settings.

Controller support should be implemented and tested before release rather than advertised as an unsupported feature.

# PART 28 — FINAL CREATIVE DIRECTION

Venting & Vending should never lose sight of its original identity.

It is not simply a sci-fi crafting game.

It is a game about starting with absolutely nothing, exploiting an absurd universe and slowly building something much bigger than yourself.

The player should begin in a disgusting ventilation shaft with a terrible machine and a handful of questionable ingredients.

They should finish with an enormous spaceship, automated factories, strange alien employees, wealthy customers and an operation that would make the Galactic Federation deeply uncomfortable.

The humour comes from the world.

The satisfaction comes from the gameplay.

The progression comes from the business.

The tension comes from the risk.

The identity comes from the contrast between the tiny, filthy maintenance tunnels and the enormous, ridiculous universe surrounding them.

**Venting & Vending should make players feel like they are building an empire in the most hilariously inappropriate way possible.**
